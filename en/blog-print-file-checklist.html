Before You Send Your File to the Printer: a 6-Point Checklist for Print Without Surprises

How to prepare a design file for print: CMYK colour, 300 DPI, 3 mm bleed, safe margins, embedded fonts and a proof, with a table of common sizes, paper weights and finishes.

Last updated: 30 October 2026 5 min read The Elreda Advertising team
HomeBlogPrinting

The 30-second summary

  • Most print problems come from the file, not the printer: dull colours, pixelated images, cut-off text, or a font that changed.
  • Before sending, check 6 things: CMYK, 300 DPI, 3 mm bleed, a safe margin, embedded fonts and a proof.
  • Send the file as a print-ready PDF, at the final size plus bleed.
  • Ask for a proof and review it before printing the full run, because correcting a proof costs far less than reprinting.

Why does print look different from the design on screen?

A screen shows colour with light; a press builds it with ink on paper. A screen shows the image at a size that is comfortable for the eye, while print exposes every pixel. That is why a design can look perfect on a phone and come off the press with different colours, a blurry photo, or a white edge on one side of the card.

This checklist collects the checks we run on every file before it goes to press. Follow it and you will avoid most surprises.

1. Is the file in CMYK, not RGB?

Screens work in RGB (red, green, blue), and presses work in CMYK (cyan, magenta, yellow, black). Some bright RGB colours, especially neon green, electric blue and strong orange, cannot be reproduced with ink, so they print dull.

The fix: design in CMYK from the start or convert the file before sending, and check the colours after conversion. For small black text use 100% black (K) only, not a black made from all four inks, so no coloured fringes appear around the letters.

2. Are the images 300 DPI at the actual size?

Resolution is the number of dots per inch. A screen needs 72, but print viewed up close (cards, brochures, menus) needs 300 dots per inch at the size it will be printed. WhatsApp photos, screenshots and images taken from social media are compressed and low-resolution, so they print pixelated.

A quick formula: an image printed 10 cm wide at 300 DPI needs about 1,181 pixels of width (10 ÷ 2.54 × 300). Large prints viewed from a distance, such as roll-ups and signs, can use a lower resolution, usually 100 to 150 DPI at actual size.

3. Did you add 3 mm of bleed?

The guillotine does not cut exactly on the line every time, and can drift by a fraction of a millimetre. If the background ends exactly at the edge of the design, a thin white line will appear on the edge of some copies.

The fix: extend backgrounds, images and colours that touch the edge 3 mm beyond the design's edges on every side. A card with a final size of 90 × 50 mm is sent at 96 × 56 mm, with crop marks.

4. Is the text kept away from the edge with a safe margin?

The same cutting drift that shows a white edge can also clip part of a phone number or a letter of the name if it sits too close to the edge.

The fix: keep a safe margin of 3 to 5 mm inside the design's edges, and place no text, logo or important element in it.

5. Are the fonts embedded in the file?

If you send a design file that relies on a font the printer does not have, the software swaps in another font automatically, which can break Arabic letters apart or change the whole design.

The fix: send a print-ready PDF (preferably PDF/X) with fonts embedded, or convert text to outlines before exporting. Always keep the editable source file yourself.

6. Did you ask for a proof before the full run?

One typo in the phone number on 1,000 cards means 1,000 cards you cannot use. A proof also shows how the colours look on the real paper, which a screen cannot.

The fix: ask for a printed proof, or at least a final PDF proof, and check: names, numbers, addresses, email, print orientation (front and back) and colours. Only after your approval does the full run start.

Which paper and finish should you choose?

UseCommon paper weightNotes
Letterhead80 – 100 gsmRuns easily through office printers
Flyers and brochures115 – 170 gsmHeavier paper feels higher quality
Catalogue or menu cover250 – 300 gsmOften laminated for protection
Business card300 – 350 gsmMatt or gloss finish, with optional spot UV

Matt lamination gives a calm, premium look and does not reflect light. Gloss lamination makes colours look stronger. Spot UV is a glossy layer on one area (such as the logo) over a matt surface, which gives a distinctive contrast to the eye and to the touch.

Final checklist before sending

  • CMYK colour mode, with black text in 100% black (K).
  • All images at 300 DPI at actual size (or 100 to 150 DPI for large prints).
  • 3 mm bleed on every side, with crop marks shown.
  • No text or important elements in the last 3 to 5 mm from the edge.
  • A print-ready PDF with fonts embedded or converted to outlines.
  • Names, numbers and addresses checked letter by letter.
  • An approved proof before printing the full run.

Frequently asked questions

Why did the printed colours come out different from the screen?

Usually because the file was in RGB, or because the screen itself is not calibrated. Some screen colours cannot be printed with ink, and the paper type and lamination also change how a colour looks. A printed proof is the only way to see the real colour before the full run.

Can a design made in Canva be printed?

Yes, as long as you export it as "PDF Print" with crop marks and bleed turned on, and use images with enough resolution. Check the colours after printing, because Canva designs mainly in RGB.

What is the best format to send to the printer?

A print-ready PDF (preferably PDF/X) at the final size plus bleed, with fonts embedded. Avoid sending JPGs or screenshots of designs that contain text.

What does bleed mean, in short?

It is an extra area, usually 3 mm, where the background extends beyond the design's edges on every side, so no white edge appears if the cut drifts slightly.

Do I need a proof for every print order?

For new orders, large runs or colours that matter to your brand: yes. For a reprint of the same approved design on the same paper, reviewing a PDF can be enough.
